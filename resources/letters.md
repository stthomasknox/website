# July 2026 – Introduction
## July 12 – An Unexpected Introduction
I wasn’t expecting to write a letter for the bulletin this weekend, but it turns out I did need to put this Sunday’s bulletin together after all! For now, therefore, let me just give a short biography to begin to introduce myself.

My name is Fr. Jordan Fetcko, but you can call me Father Jordan; my last name is hard to pronounce (and it was probably originally spelled Fetčko). That surname is Slovak, while my mother’s side of the family are mostly German. I was born and raised in Crown Point, the oldest of three. My home parish was St. Mary’s in Crown Point, and I went to the parochial school there and was an active altar server. I began to seriously discern God’s call to the priesthood in my senior year of high school and ultimately ended up entering seminary for my freshman year of college. After four years at Immaculate Heart of Mary Seminary (Winona, MN) and four more at Mundelein Seminary (Mundelein, IL), I was ordained a priest on June 6, 2015. By that point I had three more siblings: girls my parents had adopted from China, making me now the oldest of six!

My assignments until now have been as an associate pastor: first at St. Casimir and All Saints in Hammond, then St. Thomas More in Munster, then what is now Mary Mother of Mercy in Michigan City. Now, as of July 1, I’m your administrator—and I couldn’t be more excited!

**Fr. Jordan**

## July 19 – Introduction, Part 2: My “Vocation Story”
A common question every priest hears goes along the lines of, “How did you know God was calling you to be a priest?” In seminary (“priest school”) we typically referred to the response to this question as our “vocation story”—so here’s mine.

Although I grew up in a practicing Catholic family, I never gave the priesthood much thought as a real possibility for me as a child (although I did “play Mass” once or twice!). I did serve as an altar server at my local parish, which gave me a fascinating “behind-the-scenes” look at the Mass and opportunities to see and speak with priests outside of the liturgy; I realize now that already God was planting the seeds of my vocation at that time. In my senior year of high school God finally got through to me, and I began seriously discerning a potential calling to the priesthood.

At the time I had discovered and fostered a great love of learning and teaching, and so I was certain that I was called to be a teacher of some kind. In my prayer I quickly realized that a priest is a teacher in many ways: through his preaching, his counsel, and his way of life. Therefore, I got in touch with the vocations director of the Diocese of Gary and entered seminary a few months later, in the fall of 2007.

Although many seminarians discern that they are *not* called to the priesthood, for me each of my eight years of seminary only strengthened the sense that God was beckoning me to serve at his altar—and my eleven years as a priest so far have continued to affirm that I’m exactly where God wants me to be!

**Fr. Jordan**

## July 26 – My Experience of Priestly Life & Ministry
Although I may break from this pattern from time to time, I’m planning on devoting the bulletin articles of each month to a particular overarching theme, so this week I’ll wrap up my “personal introduction” theme by summarizing what the past eleven years of priesthood have been like for me.

On one level, being a priest isn’t easy! I’ve encountered broken people of all types, both in and outside of the confessional. In these and in many other situations, I’ve felt powerless to do much (or anything) to help bring healing and peace. As a priest, words and gestures that are welcomed by some are criticized by others. Living alone (as so many parish priests do nowadays) can lead to isolation and depression. When a priest picks up the phone or gets an email, only God knows what he’s about to deal with!

However, all these things are so insignificant compared to the graces of the Sacrament of Holy Orders, which have not only enabled me to navigate all these challenges and more but (perhaps even more importantly) have taught me that it’s not all on my shoulders. Whatever advice I could give to a penitent is meaningless next to the infinite mercy of God they will receive in the Sacrament of Reconciliation. I also would have gotten nowhere without the support of family and friends, generous parishioners, brother priests, and fatherly bishops. The joy of celebrating Mass, taking part in a liturgy which transcends all of time and space to make the sacrificial love of Christ present to us once more, cannot be understated, nor can I underestimate the power of the Holy Spirit to inform my ministry and preaching (if I let Him!). The seeds that I or any of us plant here will bear fruit in eternal life, but only because God gives them the life they need to grow.

All in all, my sense that God has truly called me to serve Him as a priest has only strengthened since I was ordained in 2015. I very much appreciate your prayers for me, for my brother priests and deacons, and for our bishop as we continue our ministry, so full of challenges but also blessings.

**Fr. Jordan**

# August 2026 – The Order of Preachers
## August 2 – St. Dominic & His Order
Since August 8 is the feast of St. Dominic, founder of the Order of Preachers (Dominicans)—the same Order of which St. Thomas Aquinas was a member—and since I, too, am affiliated with the Dominicans as a member of the Priestly Fraternities of St. Dominic, I plan to dedicate August’s articles to all things Dominican.

So first: Who is St. Dominic? Born in Caleruega (in modern-day Spain) in 1170, Dominic was raised and educated as a member of a fairly wealthy family, but his willingness to abandon worldly possessions was made clear when he sold his books (rare and valuable items in that era) to feed the hungry during time of famine. He was ordained a priest as a young man and became a *canon* (a type of priest who lives in community at a cathedral), giving young Dominic a sense of the value (and challenges!) of brothers living in common.

Once, when on a journey, Dominic encountered firsthand the successes of a heretical group called the Cathars, who lived humbly and denounced the comparatively sumptuous lifestyles of many priests and bishops. This inspired Dominic to pursue a greater spirit of poverty himself and also to encourage others to do so, proclaiming Jesus Christ—the original poor preacher—and practicing what they preached.

This initially small band of like-minded men quickly grew, and in 1216 Dominic received official approval from Pope Innocent III to form the Order of Preachers. His example of humility, constant prayer, and joyful preaching were a great inspiration to his brothers until he died in 1221, at the age of fifty-one. (His immediate successor as Master of the Order, by the way, was Bl. Jordan of Saxony.) He was canonized only thirteen years later. St. Dominic, pray for us!

**Fr. Jordan**

## August 9 – The Life & Charism of the Order of Preachers
Last week I went over the life and holiness of St. Dominic, founder of the Order of Preachers—a religious order that claims St. Thomas Aquinas as its most celebrated member. Today I’d like to begin to summarize the life of the Dominican Order: not just what they *do* but who they *are* in relationship to God and the Church’s mission.

Every religious order claims one or more *charisms,* particular gifts of grace that animate their life and ministry. The Dominican charism is often summarized simply as “preaching and the salvation of souls,” elaborated upon by naming the “four pillars” of Dominican life: prayer, community, study, and preaching. Ultimately, the goal of those in the Order of Preachers is to imitate the poor preacher *par excellence,* Jesus Christ, and to participate in His saving work, and the charism of the Order assists them uniquely in attaining that end. Through their **prayer** and contemplation, including communal praying of the Liturgy of the Hours, they come to know God more fully; their life in **community,** including a lifestyle of poverty in which all things are held in common, strengthens them in selflessness and charity; their **study,** which they practice as another form of prayer, they encounter the Truth, Who is Jesus Christ; and, of course, their **preaching** in word and deed hands on the Gospel to others.

In two weeks I’ll have more to say on Dominican life, but next week I need to outline the parts of the “Dominican Family,” including where I—and even some of you—fit into it!

**Fr. Jordan**

## August 16 – The Dominican “First,” “Second,” and “Third Orders”
If you look at the statue of St. Thomas Aquinas in our church (on the west side, next to the votive candles) you see him wearing the habit of the Dominican friars: a white tunic, scapular, and capuce beneath a black cappa. St. Thomas and other *friars*—a friar is a priest who is a member of a religious order that professes poverty—are members of the so-called “first order” of the Order of Preachers. (The traditional division of religious orders into “first,” “second,” and “third order” is mostly confusing and outdated nowadays, but the terms haven’t died out quite yet.) Dominican friars wear the white habit, live in community, and are engaged in many types of apostolic ministry, including teaching, itinerant preaching, leading retreats, and even pastoring parishes. It’s not uncommon for friars to earn multiple advanced degrees in philosophy, theology, and related fields.

However, even though the friars are traditionally called the “first order,” they were not the first form of Dominican life instituted by St. Dominic: he initially founded a community for women, tasking them with supporting the men’s preaching efforts through prayer. This older order, the so-called “second order,” encompasses the cloistered Dominican nuns. You’ve probably never seen one before, since *cloistered* nuns live their entire lives confined to a specific monastery, commited to prayer and growth in holiness.

Then there’s the “third order,” the most varied. If you’ve ever seen a woman in a white habit with a black veil, she was most likely a Dominican sister, who live together in community but who are also engaged in apostolic work “in the world”—typically teaching. (One of the most well-known of these communities in the United States is the one in Nashville.) The third order also includes the lay Dominicans, who live typical lives out in the world but who have been called to live the spirituality and charism of the Dominican Order in a particular way. The same holds true for the priests who join the Priestly Fraternities of St. Dominic, including myself: remaining a priest of the Diocese of Gary, I made profession as a Dominican “tertiary” in 2018. Last, but not least, the Dominican Family includes those who participate in particular apostolic ministries of the Order of Preachers: the Holy Name Society and the Rosary Confraternity, a.k.a. the Altar and Rosary Society, of which we have a chapter!

**Fr. Jordan**

## August 23 – The Mottos of the Order of Preachers
*Since August 8 was the feast of St. Dominic, founder of the religious order St. Thomas Aquinas is a member of, my letters this month have focused on the Order of Preachers.*

The religious order St. Dominic founded claims three “mottos,” all of which are Latin words or phrases that succinctly describe their identitiy and mission. Let’s look over them and see what they tell us about the members of the Dominican Family.

**Laudare, Benedicere, Prædicare (“To Praise, to Bless, to Preach”)**
Naturally, this motto of the Order of Preachers mentions the act of preaching, but only after listing the acts of praising and blessing—the latter of which literally means “to speak well of” in Latin. Dominicans are dedicated to praising and “speaking well of” God in their prayer and study, and this informs the nature of their preaching: revealing God’s goodness to the world.

**Veritas (“Truth”)**
This one-word motto must not be misunderstood. Although Dominicans are dedicated to study, and St. Thomas Aquinas is certainly well known for his lengthy exposition of even minor theological details, we mustn’t fall into the error of imagining “truth” as a mere list of correct facts, much less as a weapon to beat our enemies over the head with. Truth is a Person, Jesus Christ, and so Dominicans see study as coming to know and love God more fully.

**Contemplare et Contemplata Aliis Tradere (“To Contemplate and Hand on the Fruits of Contemplation”)**
The phrasing of this motto comes from St. Thomas Aquinas himself, who wrote, “that form of active life in which a man, by preaching and teaching, delivers to others the fruits of his contemplation, is more perfect than the life that stops at contemplation…and consequently such was the life chosen by Christ” (*Summa Theologiæ,* III, q. 40, a. 1, ad 2). This effectively summarizes the Dominican life.

Next week we’ll conclude by talking about St. Thomas Aquinas in particular and his life as a Dominican friar.

**Fr. Jordan**

## August 30 – St. Thomas Aquinas & the Order of Preachers
St. Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274) was born near Aquino in modern-day Italy (“Aquinas” is just a Latin version of *d’Aquino,* “from Aquino”), the son of Italian nobility and the youngest of nine. His education in Naples included exposure to the thought of figures who ultimately influenced his own: Aristotle (an ancient Greek), Averroes (an Andalusian Muslim), and Maimonides (a Jewish rabbi), and it was there that he was introduced to the Dominican Order, which he resolved to join. This, however, displeased his family, who went to great lengths to try and dissuade him—including holding him prisoner for a year. Eventually his mother relented and arranged his escape, and so Thomas went to Rome to meet the Master of the Order.

Sent to study in Paris, Thomas initially gained a reputation as shy or even slow, earning him the moniker “dumb ox”; however, the head of the university’s theology faculty, St. Albert the Great, remarked, “You call him the ‘dumb ox,’ but in his teaching he will one day produce such a bellowing that it will be heard throughout the world.” Indeed, both as a student and, soon after, as a teacher Thomas was a diligent and humble learner, and his written works of philosophical and theological thought began to multiply in number. These included commentaries on philosophical treatises and on Scripture, summaries and defenses of Catholic doctrine, and original essays on elements of philosophy. His most famous work is the *Summa theologiæ,* a detailed explanation of the Catholic faith in an extensive question-and-answer format.

As previously noted, however, academic study is just one form of prayer to Dominicans, and St. Thomas spent much time in silence in the presence of the Blessed Sacrament. The fruits of this contemplation included not only educational materials but also several hymns in praise of the Eucharist, most of which are still well-known today: for example, the last two verses of “Verbum supernum prodiens” and of “Pange, lingua” are traditionally used for Eucharistic Adoration (“O salutaris hostia” and “Tantum, ergo,” respectively). Later in life his prayer intensified, including multiple mystical experiences; in one of them Christ asked Thomas what sort of reward he would like for having written so many great things, and Thomas responded, *“Non nisi te, Domine”*—“Nothing but you, Lord.” On his way to the Second Council of Lyons, Thomas was injured, fell ill, and died at the age of 48.

This summary of his life and thought barely scratches the surface: his writings in particular (a sample of which can be found in English at [isidore.co/aquinas](https://isidore.co/aquinas/)) have educated and enriched Christians for centuries now, and even the Second Vatican Council continued to explicitly call for Catholic students to be instructed in Thomistic thought (cf. *Gravissimum educationis,* no. 11). I look forward to diving deeper into this beautiful mind with you in the future. St. Thomas Aquinas, patron of our parish, pray for us!

**Fr. Jordan**

# September 2026 – Four Marks of a Vibrant Parish
*The theme for September’s letters is the “Four Marks of a Vibrant Parish,” as outlined by our bishop in light of the Diocese of Gary’s pastoral planning process. I spent a recent Sunday doing a “mini retreat,” praying with and reflecting on these four marks and how they apply to our parish, and I wish to share the fruits of that reflection with you.*

## September 6 – Strong Missionary Impulse
“By their fruits you will know them” (*Mt* 7:16a). By these words we are reminded that a vibrant parish will bear good fruit, while a parish that is not vibrant will not. Of course, every parish in the world will fall somewhere in the middle, with areas that are flourishing and areas that need tending to. Today, I’d like to discuss the first “mark” of a vibrant parish: a strong missionary impulse, and what that looks like—and could look like—at St. Thomas Aquinas.

The Diocese of Gary’s vision statement is simply, “Be disciples. Be missionaries.” Applying this term *missionary* to the entire diocese is critical, because every Christian is called to be one! A missionary is not just someone who travels to distant lands to introduce foreign peoples to the Gospel; rather, he or she is someone who lets their faith imbue their life with joy and a desire to share that joy with others. If I see an amazing new film in the theater—a very rare occurrence these days, to be sure—I feel a certain impulse to tell others about it and to encourage them to go see it for themselves. Shouldn’t we feel similarly when it comes to our encounters with the very Savior of the world and His message of love? The Diocese uses an apt image for a vibrant parish: “a lighthouse—stable, joyful, and outward-facing.” A Christian community that only cares for its own is one that isn’t truly Christian, because it fails to be “the light of the world,” as Jesus described His disciples (cf. *Mt* 5:14–16).

Therefore, as our diocesan vision plan notes, the fruits of a parish with a strong missionary impulse include: 1) a culture of invitation, encounter, and accompaniment; 2) evangelization structures that seek to reach people far from the Church; 3) outreach ministries that accompany families, the poor, and the unchurched; and 4) testimonies of conversion. I invite you to reflect on how you, personally, might be called to help produce such fruits in our parish. In my brief experience at St. Thomas Aquinas so far I’ve noted that this parish already bears some of these fruits in abundance: our St. Vincent de Paul Society certainly accompanies the poor in our community, and our new Greeters Ministry is already helping to form “a culture of invitation, encounter, and accompaniment.” However, I see outreach to people outside of the Church, whether they are non-Catholics or “fallen-away” Catholics, as an area where some serious discernment and new energy are needed. Please do not hesitate to let me know your own thoughts and feedback on this and any of my letters; after all, you are the “boots on the ground”!

**Fr. Jordan**

## September 13 – Widespread Spiritual Maturity
“By their fruits you will know them” (*Mt* 7:16a). Just like with individual Christians, the more spiritually lively a parish is, the more apparent and positive its effects will be. The second “mark” of a vibrant parish is a widespread spiritual maturity, and I hope you’ll take a moment to consider my thoughts on this and how it applies to the present and the future of St. Thomas Aquinas.

Just as children are expected to grow into adults and thereby to increase in physiological, intellectual, emotional, moral, and other areas of maturity, so are we meant to undergo spiritual development throughout our lives. This absolutely includes, but does not end with, receiving the Sacraments of Initiation at the appropriate times—Baptism for infants as soon as reasonably possible, and First Communion and Confirmation during primary school years. Growing in spiritual maturity also means fostering a deepening relationship with Jesus Christ through Sunday Mass attendance, prayer, reading Scripture, and encountering His mercy in confession. Faith formation, far from ending in the eighth grade, should continue intentionally throughout one’s life, and it should be more than just academic or theoretical.

Along these very lines, the Diocese of Gary’s schema lists the fruits of a widespread spiritual maturity as: 1) clear formation pathways that invite everyone into a meaningful relationship with Christ, 2) small groups or discipleship communities, 3) a sacramental culture that prepares people well, and 4) increased participation in missions, retreats, and devotions. I hope and pray that my homilies, bulletin letters, and (now) faith formation classes will bear good fruit along these lines; but it would be foolish to not acknowledge the foundations that have already been laid here thanks to our Religious Education, the Altar and Rosary Society, and our Bible study program in particular. One of my great hopes for the future is to complement the Altar and Rosary Society (typically for women) with a Holy Name Society (typically for men) and for both groups to be the two spiritual pillars of the parish. I also firmly believe that fostering proper, appropriate reverence for the sacred is contagious—drawing oneself and others closer to Christ and to His Sacraments. From all these things will naturally (or rather, supernaturally) grow a more active devotional life and interest in special opportunities for prayer. After all, the more we love God, the more we want to get to know Him better!

I continue to welcome any feedback, comments, questions, suggestions, and ideas you may have to offer me, especially regarding these four marks of a vibrant parish and how you see them (actually or potentially) at St. Thomas Aquinas. What I’ve learned so far about this community, I’ve learned from you!

**Fr. Jordan**

## September 20 – Transformative Sunday Worship
“By their fruits you will know them” (*Mt* 7:16a). So far we’ve covered two of the four fruits, or “marks,” of a vibrant parish: a strong missionary impulse and a widespread spiritual maturity. The third of these marks is “transformative Sunday worship”; why is this important, what does this look like in general, and how might this be realized more fully at St. Thomas Aquinas?

As I went to great lengths to illustrate in my first class for *Fides,* our Lord Jesus Christ has handed on to us so much more than just a divine message, a list of truths, or a way of moral living. As important as these things are, they would be unknowable and unable to be lived if not for something else Jesus left us: the Sacraments, powerful sources of grace and means of salvation which have been safeguarded by the Apostles and their successors, the bishops, for the past two millennia. Baptism, of course, is primary, and it is the “gateway” to the other Sacraments; among those other Sacraments, the Eucharist is absolutely the most essential. As Pope Benedict XVI plainly stated, “Without the Eucharist the Church quite simply would not exist” (*Angelus,* 26 Jun 2011): there would be no gathering of the Body of Christ to enter into the mysteries of our redemption, no clear means of unity among Christ’s followers, no reliable source of grace to strengthen His disciples as they strive to live the Christian life. Now, to be certain, the Real Presence of our Lord in the Eucharist can be found even in the most “lazy” or irreverent Masses, but it is equally certain that every liturgy celebrated with respect for God and the sacred will accomplish the mission of the Eucharist more fully.

This last point is why the Diocese of Gary names the following as fruits of transformative Sunday worship: 1) beautiful liturgy and sacred music, 2) warm hospitality and connection, 3) inspired preaching that ignites the faithful to go forth on mission, and 4) a sense of spiritual “home.” Last week my letter expressed my desire to re-form a Holy Name Society at St. Thomas Aquinas, and I envision this and the Altar and Rosary Society as being two great “arms,” supporting liturgical worship and devotional prayer in our parish. In the near future I will also offer liturgical minister training (or re-training) for all interested, with an emphasis on the ways in which ministers can foster and model reverence for the Eucharistic mysteries. Perhaps more distant goals include a music ministry for cantors or even a choir (I hear some very good singers in the pews during Mass!), as well as planning more opportunities for prayer, especially within the context of Eucharistic Adoration, to better foster the feeling that this church is truly your spiritual home.

I hope my letters this month are inspiring you to ponder these same matters with me. If you have any desire to help out in a particular way—or even if you want to help but don’t know how you can!—please let me know, and perhaps I can help you find exactly how God is inviting you to build up this community. After all, this isn’t my parish—it’s yours! We’ll explore that reality more in next Sunday’s bulletin…

**Fr. Jordan**

## September 27 – Well-developed Shared Leadership
“By their fruits you will know them” (*Mt* 7:16a). If you judge St. Thomas Aquinas Parish by its fruits, would you consider this to be a vibrant parish? As we’ve seen throughout this month’s series, there are definitely many fruits here which should prompt an enthusiastic “Yes!” in response, but there are also areas where we have the opportunity to do a lot more tending to the plant, so to speak. Let’s wrap up by turning to the fourth “mark” of a vibrant parish, well-developed shared leadership.

A parish community should never be a priest’s “kingdom” or fiefdom. On the one hand, throughout the ages God has made it abundantly clear that leaders are called to shepherd and to serve with humility; simultaneously, a priest should never be expected to accomplish everything by himself! Although the headship of a pastor (or administrator) should be respected, the parish itself, in all senses of the term, is not his property but rather the shared patrimony of all its members. To put it bluntly, without your stewardship this parish would not exist or endure; some Catholics in our diocese have already had to confront the reality that a priest alone can’t keep the lights on if the parish can’t even afford to pay the electricity bill. This, of course, is far more than a question of money; any efforts that aim to advance the mission of the Gospel need faithful people to help carry them out. Indeed, “The harvest is abundant but the laborers are few” (*Mt* 9:37)!

What does the Diocese of Gary list as fruits of well-developed shared leadership? Those would be 1) clarity of roles and responsibilities, 2) a healthy, collaborative culture, 3) well-formed lay leaders who support the pastor, and 4) structures that support accountability and mission fulfillment. At St. Thomas Aquinas or literally any other parish, more help is needed—but this should not mean that the same ten people need to *do more* but rather that we need *more people* to embrace true stewardship of their spiritual home. This absolutely places upon me, first and foremost, a responsibility to ensure “clarity of roles” and a “collaborative culture,” and I can assure you I’m working hard to accomplish this. However, I would be remiss to not acknowledge just how much this community already lives a spirit of shared leadership. I would need several more pages to recount all the ways in which I’ve witnessed this already, so suffice it to say that the—there’s no better word for it—*heroic* generosity of many of you proclaims loudly and clearly your love for this parish. You inspire me to do these sorts of reflections, looking ahead to the future with excitement and optimism, precisely because I know I won’t be blazing this trail alone.

As before, I welcome any feedback; part of well-developed shared leadership is that this sort of communication goes both ways!

**Fr. Jordan**

# October 2026 – The Sanctification of Time
*Time is a precious gift from God. Time not only gives us the opportunity to repent and to grow in knowledge and love of God—it also gives us a foretaste of eternity: the cycles of the hours, days, months, and seasons repeat and unfold without end, inviting us ever more deeply into their beauty. In this month’s letters I’d like to explain the Catholic traditions which can help us truly sanctify each moment of this our pilgrimage here on Earth.*

## October 4 – The Hours of the Day
“Seven times a day I praise you for your just decrees” (Ps 119 \[118]:164). From the “morning offering” to the classic “Now I lay me down to sleep,” many traditional prayers have been used throughout the centuries to help Catholics live every moment of the day in the presence of God. No such prayer, however, is as ancient or as emblematic of the prayer of the Church than the Liturgy of the Hours, also called the Divine Office. With its origins in the aforementioned Jewish practice of praising God “seven times a day” with psalms and Scripture readings, the Liturgy of the Hours has always been placed alongside the Eucharist in terms of importance in the life of the Church.

The history of the Divine Office would fill this bulletin, but for the sake of getting right to the point, here’s how the seven “Hours” of the Liturgy of the Hours serve to sanctify the day and night:
- The *Office of Readings* (formerly *Matins*) – Traditionally prayed during the night (often at 3 AM), this more lengthy Hour features two longer readings—one from Scripture and the other from the writings of a Church Father or another Saint, reinforcing the continuity between the living Word of God in times both old and new.
- *Lauds* (Morning Prayer) – One of the two essential “hinge Hours,” Lauds begins the day with joyful praise, recalling the Resurrection of Christ, which also took place at dawn. This prayer always includes the praying of the *Benedictus* (*Lk* 1:68–79), in which Zechariah hails the birth of his son, John the Baptist, who heralds the Dawn of our salvation, Jesus Christ.
- Daytime Prayer: *Terce,* *Sext,* and *None* – Although only one of these three “minor Hours” is typically prayed nowadays, these prayers—named for the third, sixth, and ninth hour of the day (9 AM, noon, and 3 PM)—commemorate events surrounding the Crucifixion. (Scripture and tradition indicate that Jesus took up His Cross at 9 AM, was crucified at noon, and died at 3 PM.)
- *Vespers* (Evening Prayer) – This other “hinge Hour” calls to mind the evening of each of the days of Creation, asking God to bless the work we have completed and to accompany us throughout the darkness of the night to come. The *Magnificat* (*Lk* 1:46–55), a consistent feature of this Hour, reminds us of the enduring works of God as we echo Mary’s faithful song of praise.
- *Compline* (Night Prayer) – Typically prayed right before bedtime, this brief Hour sends us off to bed with faith that God will be our light in the darkness of night—and at the end of our earthly life.

Although the Second Vatican Council recommended the praying of at least part of the Divine Office to laypersons (not just to clergy and religious, who are obligated to pray it), this call has yet to be widely answered—mainly because most parishes have yet to heed the Council’s instruction to pray Lauds and/or Vespers on Sundays. In the months and years to come, trust that I will strive to remedy this!

**Fr. Jordan**

## October 11 – The Days of the Week
“Teach us to number our days, that we may gain wisdom of heart” (Ps 90 \[89]:12). As someone who has been fascinated by space, space travel, astronomy, and astrophysics since childhood, I’ve always loved the ancient association between the days of the week and the classical celestial bodies (Sunday = Sun, Monday = Moon, Tuesday = Mars, etc.); but did you know that Catholics do something similar?
- **Sunday** – the Holy Trinity (and, of course, the Resurrection)
- **Monday** – the Holy Angels
- **Tuesday** – the Apostles
- **Wednesday** – St. Joseph
- **Thursday** – the Holy Eucharist
- **Friday** – the Passion of the Lord
- **Saturday** – the Blessed Virgin Mary

Some of these associations (and the reasons for them) are well-known: Friday, for example, has always been a day for penance (especially abstinence from meat) due to Christ’s sanctifying Death taking place on that day of the week. Other connections are less certain, but Thursday through Sunday, in particular, truly can be an extended celebration of the Paschal Mystery each and every week, as we pass through the Upper Room to Calvary and then the empty tomb.

Catholics might choose to sanctify each day of the week by recalling these traditional dedications in various ways, through fitting prayers (pre-written or off-the-cuff), works of mercy, or penances. Always keep in mind, however, that the Lord’s Day, Sunday, and its celebration of the Resurrection is and has always been the principal focus of the week! Each Sunday—and each Sunday Mass—is a sort of mini-Easter, a culmination of all the mysteries of our faith. Look back at the list of days above, and you won’t find a single person or event that isn’t mystically present in the Eucharist. I pray that all of us may give Sunday and the Sunday Eucharist the proper pride of place it deserves in our lives as Christians, so that the rest of our week, too, may be made holy.

**Fr. Jordan**

## October 18 – The Months of the Year
“The moon that marks the chan­ging times, gov­ern­ing the sea­sons, their last­ing sign, by which we know the feast days and fixed dates, this light-giver which wanes in its course: as its name says, each month it re­news it­self; how won­drous in this change!” (*Sir* 43:6–8). Like in Hebrew, our English word month comes from moon—although our modern months no longer follow the phases of the Moon. As with the days of the week, Catholics over the centuries have come to associate each month with a certain devotion, often drawn from a significant feast occurring in that month:
- **January** – the Holy Name of Jesus
- **February** – the Holy Family
- **March** – St. Joseph
- **April** – the Holy Eucharist
- **May** – the Blessed Virgin Mary
- **June** – the Sacred Heart of Jesus
- **July** – the Precious Blood of Jesus
- **August** – the Immaculate Heart of Mary
- **September** – the Seven Sorrows of Mary
- **October** – the Holy Rosary
- **November** – the Holy Souls in Purgatory
- **December** – the Immaculate Conception of Mary

Each of the persons or mysteries enumerated above have enjoyed popular devotion from Catholics over the centuries. Explaining the history of devotions in the Church would take far more space than I have available here, but I do want to observe that our Church is currently in an era of lessened devotion, in the aftermath of Vatican II. I would characterize this as something of an “overcorrection” of former Catholic attitudes toward devotions—that one must perform acts of piety in order to “earn” entrance into heaven. (I don’t mean to imply that all Catholics thought this way, but such ideas and assumptions were once widespread.) Today, however, we are witnessing among many Catholics a “rediscovery” of devotions; if you wish to be among those searching the Church’s treasure trove for aids to grow in love of God and in holiness, perhaps the list above can serve as a basic map.

For example, many litanies exist in our tradition, many of which are dedicated to the above objects of devotion, including the Litany of the Holy Name of Jesus, the Litany of the Sacred Heart of Jesus, and the Litany of the Blessed Virgin Mary (the “Litany of Loreto”). These prayers use varying invocations, ending with a consistent response, such as “pray for us” or “have mercy on us,” exploring the depths of the mysteries of our faith while also reinforcing our desperate need for divine assistance. I’ve already spoken at length about the Rosary, which is the devotional focus of this current month. Next month’s bulletin letters will cover, in part, the beautiful practice of praying for the dead; one devotional way of doing so during the month of November may be to visit a cemetery or specifically the grave of a loved one; a prayer ritual can be found in the Book of Blessings which I will excerpt for anyone who would like to use it.

In the weeks, months, and years to come I hope to provide all sorts of resources to help individuals, families, and this parish as a whole to explore the rich devotional life of our Church—in which we find nothing more and nothing less than aids for our salvation!

**Fr. Jordan**

## October 25 – Seasons and Holy-days
“\[God] causes the changes of the times and seasons” (*Dn* 2:21). As the days get shorter, the nights lengthen, the air becomes chilly, and crops are harvested, human beings have customarily begun to think more about death. For 95 percent of the history of human civilization, our lives have revolved around agriculture: planting seeds in the spring, harvesting in the fall, and taking shelter from the deadly cold in the winter. Each autumn humans worked and prayed so that the harvest would be bountiful and they’d have enough food stored up to get them through the cold months. Would there be enough? Would everyone survive? These were very real questions every year.

This is why the cycle of the year is intimately connected with the mysteries of the life, Death, and Resurrection of Christ—God Who became man, entering into a world so full of death and darkness, “who destroyed death and brought life and immortality to light through the Gospel” (*2 Tm* 1:10). Keeping this connection in mind, this brief tour of the seasons of the liturgical year should make more sense:
- In the darkness of late autumn we celebrate the new year with the beginning of **Advent** (on the Sunday nearest the Feast of St. Andrew), finding hope in the promised coming of our Savior (and indeed, the season’s name is Latin for “coming”).
- As soon as the days begin to lengthen once more, we rejoice at the birth of Jesus Christ, the true Light Who has entered into the world to drive away the darkness of sin and death, in the brief season known in English as **Christmas** (from “Christ-Mass”).
- The date of Easter Sunday—the first Sunday after the first full moon after the spring equinox—determines the rest of the liturgical year, just as our entire faith revolves around the Resurrection of Christ from the dead. Forty days (not counting Sundays) before that all-important Sunday begins the Season of **Lent** (which in Old English literally just means “spring”), which imitates Jesus’ forty days of fasting in the desert.
- From the evening of Holy Thursday through Easter Sunday evening—a period of only three days—we observe the **Sacred Paschal Triduum** (literally “three days”), intensely commemorating the central events of our faith.
- Easter Sunday, in turn, kicks off the fifty-day **Easter Season,** during which we celebrate the new life Jesus has given us, just as new life returns to the world around us: indeed, in churches flowers now abound—while during Lent they were absent.

(The rest of the liturgical year is nowadays known as **Ordinary Time,** not because those weeks are plain or unremarkable but simply because they are ordered/numbered.) Thus is our entire year sanctified, in one great festival of praise for all God has done for us.

**Fr. Jordan**

# November 2026 – The Theology of Death

## November 1 – Prayer for the Dead and All Souls Day
To put it bluntly: November is a month of death. The darkness of night overwhelms our waking hours, the temperature drops, leaves turn brown and fall away, living creatures go into hibernation or migrate elsewhere. As I noted in another recent letter, for all of pre-industrial human history these colder months were literally deadly, full of unfortunate fatalities through starvation and freezing. This is why there is a close link between harvest festivals and death, between this time of year and thinking about the “beyond.” As fellow human beings we join in this reflection, but as fellow Christians we do so with faith in the resurrection, with hope in eternal life, and with love for those who have gone before us.

The seventh and last of the Spiritual Works of Mercy is to “pray for the living and the dead.” St. Paul provides an example of this in one of his letters, saying of a fellow Christian, “May the Lord grant him to find mercy from the Lord on that day” (*2 Tm* 1:18)—“that day” meaning the Day of Judgment at the end of his earthly life and of all time (see the upcoming November 22 bulletin for more about that). When we pray for the dead we pray for mercy, for God’s love to purify those who have died and prepare them to enter into His presence. This state or period of purification is known as Purgatory, which I will explain in the November 15 bulletin; but for now suffice it to say that, for us who are still alive here on Earth, death does not terminate our love for the departed. Just as we willed the good of our loved ones while they yet lived, so do we continue to desire blessings and blessedness for them. This impulse to keep our deceased loved ones in our thoughts and in our prayers is not only natural—it’s supernatural.

Catholic tradition gives us a spiritually healthy outlet for this impulse on November 2 in particular: after we celebrate the supreme joy of the blessed souls in heaven on All Saints Day and ask them to pray for us, we then turn to the remainder of the faithful departed, those who have gone before us but who may still need us to pray for them. Although the faithful departed were remembered on various days in the early Church, the modern date of November 2 spread across Christendom during the Middle Ages. Dominican friars began a tradition wherein they offer three Masses on All Souls Day for the dead; this practice was made a universal custom during World War I, remaining to this day in the modern Missal, which says, “On this day, any Priest may celebrate three Masses.” One Mass is to be offered for a particular intention, another for all the faithful departed, and the third is specifically for the souls of those who have died in war (recall the WWI origins). As with all Mass offerings, this applies the infinite merits of the saving Death of Christ to these particular intentions.

Another common custom on All Souls Day or the following days is to prayerfully visit a cemetery. We will do so after the morning Mass on November 2, praying at Crown Hill Cemetery, and printouts are available for anyone who would like to do their own “prayer service” this month.

## November 8 – Christian Funeral Q&A
**Q: What is meant by a “Christian funeral”?**  
A: As Christians, we believe that human beings were made for eternal life, that we lost that destiny through original sin, and that the Death and Resurrection of Jesus Christ reopened the way for us. As the introduction to the *Order of Christian Funerals* states, “The Church’s liturgical and sacramental life and proclamation of the Gospel make this mystery present in the life of the faithful. Through the Sacraments of Baptism, Confirmation, and Eucharist, men and women are initiated into this mystery” (no. 2). Therefore, for those who have received the pledge of eternal life it is most fitting to mark the end of their earthly life with prayer and liturgy. Most essentially, “the Mass, the memorial of Christ’s Death and Resurrection, is the principal celebration of the Christian funeral” (no. 5), but a Christian funeral encompasses more than this. The fullest expression of prayer for the deceased comprises three “stations”: a prayer vigil, the funeral Mass, and the Rite of Committal at the cemetery. Every baptized Catholic—with a limited few serious exceptions (see *Code of Canon Law,* c. 1184)—has the right to a Christian funeral, as do catechumens who die before receiving the Sacraments of Initiation.

**Q: What takes place at a funeral Mass that distinguishes it from a typical Mass, and what do these extra actions signify?**  
A: Everything unique to the funeral Mass serves three purposes: 1) to give a reminder of the deceased person’s baptism, 2) to proclaim the sanctity of the human body, even after death, and 3) to give the Church the opportunity to pray for the deceased. When the earthly remains of the deceased are brought into the church, the priest celebrant receives the funeral party near the entrance, and he sprinkles the casket or urn with holy water; the casket is then covered in a white cloth and processed into church, where it is stationed near the lit paschal candle. All of these symbols are strongly reminiscent of the Rite of Baptism. Incense is traditionally used at least at the Offertory of funeral Masses, giving the faithful additional time for prayer and a symbol of our prayers rising like smoke toward the heavens. In place of the final blessing and dismissal a sign of farewell is given, as the casket or urn is incensed just as the altar was, demonstrating the reverence we should show to the body. The deceased’s earthly remains then occupy a place of honor in a procession to the cemetery.

**Q: I understand that the Church used to forbid cremation but allows it now. Is this true?**  
A: Yes, although the reality is more complicated than that. Cremation was practiced among some of the first Christians, but it quickly became frowned upon, as Roman persecutors began burning the bodies of the Christians they killed, thinking this could somehow kill their souls and prevent entrance into heaven. Around the time of the Second Vatican Council cremation was permitted once more, but note how this is expressed in the modern Code of Canon Law: “The Church earnestly recommends that the pious custom of burying the bodies of the deceased be observed; nevertheless, the Church does not prohibit cremation unless it was chosen for reasons contrary to Christian doctrine” (c. 1176, §3). These erroneous beliefs might include “considering death as the definitive annihilation of the person, or the moment of fusion with Mother Nature or the universe, or as a stage in the cycle of regeneration, or as the definitive liberation from the ‘prison’ of the body” (Instruction *Ad resurgendum cum Christo,* no. 3). Burial of an intact body more clearly expresses our belief in the resurrection of the body at the end of time—though, to be clear, it is not our earthly body that will be resurrected, and therefore cremation will not prevent this from occurring. Whether the body is cremated or not, internment in a cemetery or other dedicated, sacred place is necessary, respecting the integrity of the body and encouraging all the faithful still living to pray for our deceased loved ones and for all the dead.

**Fr. Jordan**

## November 15 – Purgatory Explained
> “Judas \[Maccabeus] and his companions went to gather up the bodies of the fallen and bury them with their kindred in their ancestral tombs. But under the tunic of each of the dead they found amulets sacred to the idols of Jamnia, which the law forbids the Jews to wear.… Turning to supplication, they prayed that the sinful deed might be fully blotted out.… He then took up a collection among all his soldiers, amounting to two thousand silver drachmas, which he sent to Jerusalem to provide for an expiatory sacrifice. In doing this he acted in a very excellent and noble way, inasmuch as he had the resurrection in mind; for if he were not expecting the fallen to rise again, it would have been superfluous and foolish to pray for the dead. But if he did this with a view to the splendid reward that awaits those who had gone to rest in godliness, it was a holy and pious thought. Thus he made atonement for the dead that they might be absolved from their sin.” (*2 Mc* 12:38–40ab, 42a, 43–46)

Like the fallen soldiers in this passage, we are all sinners—from time to time placing something, some “idol,” above God, and many of us (most of us?) die with some level of attachment to not-God weighing down our souls. Following the testimony of Scripture (see, for example, *2 Samuel* 12:13–14) and of the human experience, Catholics believe that our sins incur two types of punishment: *eternal punishment* and *temporal punishment.* “Eternal punishment,” frankly, means Hell, that state of eternal separation from God; “temporal punishment” means the effects of sin which we experience for some set period of time. For example, if you intentionally deceive someone, in addition to potentially separating you from God (eternal punishment), the temporal punishment incurred by that sin will likely include others no longer trusting you. However, it’s very likely that we will not “pay off” all temporal punishment incurred by our sins in this life—or, put another way: it’s unlikely that we will rid ourselves of all earthly burdens before our time comes to meet God.

In light of this, God provides in abundance—both during this life and after. Several of the Sacraments have as an effect the remission of temporal punishment due to sin: Baptism and Anointing of the Sick (provided the latter is received alongside Penance) remit all eternal and temporal punishment (*Catechism of the Catholic Church,* no. 1263, 1532); the Eucharist, if received worthily, remits some temporal punishment (cf. no. 1394, 1436), as does Penance to some extent. Prayer, fasting, almsgiving, and penances of all kinds also remit temporal punishment, as do *indulgences* (a topic for another time). For those who die in a state of grace (i.e. with no eternal punishment) but whose temporal punishment remains wholly or partially “unpaid,” Purgatory is necessary.

The Catechism reads: “All who die in God’s grace and friendship, but still imperfectly purified, are indeed assured of their eternal salvation; but after death they undergo purification, so as to achieve the holiness necessary to enter the joy of heaven” (no. 1030). Many analogies have been proposed to help explain the necessity of Purgatory before Heaven, such as a filthy person allowing someone to clean him before entering into a banquet, or someone in a pitch black room needing the light to be gradually unveiled rather than illuminated all at once. To be clear: contrary to what some people think, Purgatory is not a “testing ground”—every single soul there is destined for Heaven. Christians, therefore, have always prayed for the dead (see my November 1 letter), that God’s sanctifying grace may be poured out upon them.

**Fr. Jordan**

## November 22 – The Four Last Things and the *Dies iræ*
Death, Judgment, Heaven, and Hell: these are traditionally known as the “four last things,” and the modern Liturgy of the Hours invites the faithful to contemplate these things during the final week of the liturgical year with the sequence *Dies iræ* (“Day of wrath”), given as an optional hymn for the Office of Readings, Lauds, and Vespers during the weekdays of the Thirty-Fourth Week in Ordinary Time. Dating from the 13th century (if not earlier), the *Dies iræ* used to form part of Requiem Masses (Masses for the Dead), including All Souls Day, but it was removed from the Roman Missal after Vatican II because…to be frank, it’s a gloomy chant! You definitely know the opening melodic phrase of the *Dies iræ*—it’s been used in film soundtracks galore[^1] as a musical shorthand for danger, death, and doom—and the lyrics largely match this mood. However, if read (and prayed) with an open mind I think it can provide a lot of insight into the four last things, pulling back the curtain and making this anxiety-inducing topic less frightening to us mortals.

Our judgment at the end, be it our *particular judgment* at the end of our life or the *final judgment* at the end of time, may conjure an image of a cruel, strict God who casts all those displeasing to Him into eternal fire. Indeed, referring to the day of final judgment as the “day of wrath” seems to reinforce this, as do all descriptions of the wrath of God in Scripture (e.g. *Rom* 5:9) and elsewhere. However, note that God—pure spirit and infinitely exceeding our human nature—does not, in fact, experience emotions (such as anger) and that human descriptions of God must necessarily *anthropomorphize* Him, giving Him human qualities simply so that we can attempt to understand Him. God’s “wrath,” therefore, must be read as poetic language, referring to the overwhelming power and inevitability of God’s justice. In other words, the “day of wrath” is the day on which the justice of God is fully accomplished at last. To us Christians, that should be a joyful day!

Indeed, several verses of the *Dies iræ* express great trust in the Lord and hope in His love; for example: *“King of fearsome power and glory, / freely saving all the faithful, / save me, fount of love and pity.”* After all, the justice of God is supplemented by His mercy, and so our encounter with the Judge at the end is an encounter with Love. As the radiance of this love throws into stark contrast our imperfections, how might we respond? The sequence gives a straightforward answer: *“Groaning like a guilty sinner, blushing at my faults and failings, I beseech you, God, to spare me.”* Rather than pridefully denying wrongdoing or fleeing in fear because we falsely think our sins are unforgivable, we must acknowledge our sins and seek God’s forgiveness. (Inasmuch as possible, we should seek to do this here on Earth while we have the time!)

The *Dies iræ* makes it clear what the alternative is: Hell. *“Though my prayers are all unworthy, in your goodness show me kindness, lest I burn in fire eternal.”* Note the dichotomy here: *either* God’s kindness *or* “fire eternal.” Without God’s grace at work in us, we are damned. Since Hell is, simply speaking, the state of eternal separation from God, this is a tautology: of course, without God we are without God! How, then, do we attain Heaven? Only by accepting and receiving God’s help, which is constantly offered to us in this life. Let me summarize this bluntly: God does not choose Hell for us—we do. Every damned soul had his or her chance to accept God and freely rejected that option. Therefore, may our humble prayer to God be as the final verse of the *Dies iræ*: *“God of grandeur and of glory, Trinity in loving splendor, number us among the blessed. Amen.”*

## November 29 – *Memento Mori:* “Remember Death”
At the cemetery after a certain funeral Mass, I experienced something unique and memorable—thought I’ve since learned it’s not unusual among Hispanic funerals, as this one was. After the Rite of Committal had concluded the entire group of family and friends—probably around one hundred people—remained, looking on as the casket was lowered into the ground and the lid was placed on the concrete vault. Then, to my surprise, several of the male family members stepped forward, picked up shovels, and began to dump soil into the grave—not just one shovelful, as I’ve seen done symbolically, but until the ground was filled in. I’m used to interments where the family leaves before the casket is lowered; in this case, however, the deceased’s loved ones confronted the reality and finality of death in a radically tangible way.

As we enter now into a new liturgical year and the Season of Advent, our awareness of our heavenly destiny shouldn’t be set aside: not only does Advent encourage us to look ahead to the Second Coming of Christ, but also the daily and weekly rhythm of our Christian life continues to encourage us to move forward toward the goal that is Christ Jesus (cf. *Phil* 3:14). Every Mass is a *memento mori*—a reminder that we will die—because the Eucharist is a foretaste of Heaven. Compline (Night Prayer), the simplest and briefest Hour of the Liturgy of the Hours, serves as a daily reminder of our future passing: the Gospel Canticle *Nunc dimittis* (“Now you dismiss your servant in peace…”) echoes the Prophet Simeon’s acceptance of death now that the Holy Spirit’s promise to him has been fulfilled, and the concluding versicle states, “May the all-powerful Lord grant us a restful night and *a peaceful death.*” (In Latin it’s *finem perfectum,* literally “a perfect end,” and I’m curious how the upcoming Second Edition will translate it.)

We live in a world which values ephemeral, earthly goods—money, pleasure, experiences, and the like—and so our worldly inclinations shy away from acknowledging our mortality. However, a Christian should keep death in mind: *Memento mori!* “Remember that you will die!” This attitude, though it may start as a fearful motivator to “repent and believe in the Gospel,” shouldn’t remain as such; as we grow in love of Christ we learn to say and believe, as St. Paul did, “To me life is Christ, and death is gain” (*Phil* 1:21). Our looming mortality stops being a matter of “I have to get clean and stay clean from sin so I don’t end up in Hell” and becomes “I want to be as prepared as possible to see God face-to-face and embrace Him as my Father and friend.” Or, to put it poetically and prayerfully, as in the Byzantine tradition’s “*Kontakion* of the Departed”: *“We are inflamed with ecstasy if we but hear there is light eternal there: that there is a fountain of our life and everlasting delight. There is Paradise, where every soul of the Righteous rejoices. Let us all also enter into Christ, that we all may cry out: Alleluia!”*

**Fr. Jordan**
